## Study and information boundaries

The two APIs are **RxJS and Bacon.js**, both reactive JavaScript libraries. There is no imperative/Observer treatment and no Nu, F#, game-maintenance or coding-agent participant. The source describes distinct abstractions, operator sets, syntax, error behavior and documentation, so even a reliable library difference would be a bundled API effect. Its popularity-based selection excludes a deprecated Most.js version and is not a representative sample of all reactive interfaces.

Six structural metrics are implemented with the authors' UAX tool. Exception specificity and thread safety are excluded. Function-parameter length uses a threshold of four; documentation length uses 50 words. Exported declarations and file/module boundaries determine the grouping metric. Thus module granularity, naming conventions and word counts affect these proxies. They do not measure users' understanding directly. The paper itself notes that folder-level grouping could change RxJS's weak grouping score.

The human study recruits from one distributed-applications course over three semesters with **17, 8 and 27 enrolled students**. Enrolment is not a count of consented or assigned participants. The paper says consenting students were assigned APIs randomly in balanced groups, but the available reported outcomes concern **18 retained submitters: five Bacon.js and thirteen RxJS**. Inclusion requires at least two timely task solutions. Neither the complete initial allocation nor an all-assigned outcome analysis is established by these retained records. Twelve submitters answered the questionnaire, four/eight by API; eight were invited based on willingness and prior collaboration, and six were interviewed, three/three. All four Bacon.js questionnaire respondents reported over five years' programming experience, compared with two of eight RxJS respondents. This does not establish the experience of every initially assigned participant.

Training covered reactive concepts, examples, marble diagrams and an optional RxJS learning game. Participants had three days for five related HTTP-request tasks, then three days for the questionnaire. Task execution was unsupervised and times were self-estimated. Interview accounts explicitly mention restrictions on Stack Overflow and ChatGPT. The optional game targets RxJS, so equal nominal access does not establish equivalent learning support. The first author conducted the sessions; both authors coded the interviews and resolved differences through discussion. This is not independent external review.

The task sequence adds retry/error handling, randomized requests, timed cancellation and a rolling mean to two basic request patterns. Task 4 explicitly requires a reactive termination operator and rules out an imperative unsubscribe/timer solution. Consequently, the endpoint includes a requested source convention, not only externally observable behavior. The tasks share code and training history and are not five independent application families. No controlled documentation improvement, long-term retention or professional adoption trial is conducted.

Read-only Artifact Tool extraction preserved the source workbooks. Independent JavaScript arithmetic checked all **288 response-to-score mappings**, including nine negatively worded items, and all 48 API-specific item means without a mismatch. This is aggregate reconstruction, not a native Excel or experimental reproduction.

- **Completion:** the retained participants have 90 task slots, with 55 complete, 28 incomplete and seven not done. Bacon.js has 20/25 complete, RxJS 35/65. These reproduce the reported approximately 80% versus 53.85% descriptive rates. All-five completion is 3/5 versus 2/13 in the released labels, a different endpoint from mean task completion. It remains conditional on the retained participants and has no supported causal interpretation. The published task-5 total 55.57% and task-2 RxJS 15.39% differ slightly from the direct fractions, 55.56% and 15.38%; the main distinction is the denominator, not these rounding differences.
- **Time:** published Table 5 uses 16, 15, 16, 15 and 13 timing records across the five tasks. Its 33.3 minutes is the arithmetic average of task medians 56, 40, 17.5, 30 and 23. It is neither a total per participant nor the average of all individual durations. The corresponding per-API averages of medians are about 25.8 and 39.8. The raw timing source was not reconstructed; do not infer speed on all assigned or all successful tasks from these summaries.
- **Perceived usability:** the released calculation yields Bacon.js/RxJS average-dimension scores 3.15/2.99 and an **equal-API-weight** average of 3.07. It is not a pooled mean over twelve respondents. Item scores are rounded before dimension aggregation; the dimensions contain different numbers of items. The highest dimension, reusability, is 3.75/3.43, with equal-API average 3.59. The prose's 3.75 general reusability value instead matches the Bacon.js group. Low error-proneness/operator-discovery scores coexist with positive self-reported readability and reuse.
- **Structural scores:** the artifact reports average metric scores 0.81/0.87 and their mean 0.84. Those lie in Table 3's *better* interval [0.7, 0.9), although the discussion calls overall structural usability excellent. The paper's linear rescaling of 0.84 to 4.36 does not establish that structural metrics and questionnaire responses measure the same construct. Likewise, the midpoint of a 1–5 scale is 3, although the prose once calls 2.5 its midpoint. These are interpretation limitations; they do not erase the observed usability complaints.

The narrative attributes difficulty to undocumented behavior, similar operator names, weak examples and error/state handling. These are observations and proposed explanations, not separately randomized causes. In particular, Bacon.js's error-continuation semantics can make the error task easier without establishing a general advantage of its architecture. Positive task labels can coexist with an author's criticism of source style; functional completeness and perceived code cleanliness must remain distinct. Suggestions to improve documentation, reduce effective operator complexity or add visual aids were not themselves tested interventions.

## Consequences for the Nu question

**Unique:** unconfirmed. Empirical investigation of reactive usability already exists; generic claims that functional/reactive organization aids comprehension, reuse or adoption cannot be presented as an untouched field. This study does not duplicate a specific Nu state/lifecycle mechanism or D1's exhaustive-versus-fallback coding-agent contrast.

**Valuable:** motivated but unmeasured for Nu. Retain operator discovery, learning cost, documentation quality, error recovery and state handling as credible costs and rivals alongside the earlier positive comprehension evidence. A small API or pure core alone does not establish easier maintenance. The authors' reported limitations support testing an explicit mechanism, not importing their library ranking or task times as an expected Nu effect.

**Scientifically valid:** the source reinforces explicit assignment/retention denominators, experience and task-family dependence, ordinary documentation/tool access, objective behavioral obligations and separate subjective ratings. Preserve all attempts in any later ALF study. If correctness includes a source convention, state it prospectively; otherwise allow behaviorally correct reorganizations. This audit validates none of ALF's source equivalence, oracle, apparatus or resource assumptions. Experimental allocation remains zero.

## Follow-up decisions

- S45, *Mining the usage of reactive programming APIs*, is the cited primary source for usage and developer-question prevalence. Its existing queued status is retained; S40's interview sample cannot replace it or establish field prevalence.
- **Promote a new close architecture lead:** Marum, Jones and Cunningham, *Towards a Reactive Game Engine* (2019), [DOI 10.1109/SoutheastCon42311.2019.9020527](https://doi.org/10.1109/SoutheastCon42311.2019.9020527). A two-title Scite identity search returned it and the next source. The author-hosted [eight-page PDF lead](https://john.cs.olemiss.edu/~hcc/papers/Marum_2019_IEEE_SouthEastCon.pdf) and primary abstract describe dependency-ordered game updates compared with Unity and UniRx. Methods/evaluation remain unread in this segment. It could change claims about Nu's distinctive state coordination and update-order guarantees and should be read before a broad architecture novelty claim.
- **Conditional metric-rival lead:** Venigalla and Chimalakonda, *On the comprehension of application programming interface usability in game engines*, [DOI 10.1002/spe.2985](https://doi.org/10.1002/spe.2985). Identity and abstract screen only. Promote if adopting its usability metrics or claiming prevalence of poor game-engine API usability. S40's secondary description is not a human-outcome estimate from that work.
- S39 and its earlier conference edition remain one documented empirical lineage. UAX, CDN variants, usability-sample guidance, factory-method studies and general API-design references remain conditional on adopting their measurements or making their specific claims. Neither the entire bibliography nor every cited website becomes a mandatory new read. Remaining S40 gaps are the publisher PDF/figures, exact publisher-supplement equivalence and any needed timing or initial-allocation records; they must stay explicit if the publication's numbers are reused.
