The comparison concerns multiple regression, SEM and a regression-based potential-outcome method for a binary outcome, with either a continuous or binary mediator. The exposure is normally distributed; simulation paths`a,b,c′`are each.6. There are three outcome prevalences(.1,.3,.5), giving three continuous-mediator and nine binary-mediator conditions. Each has 500 samples of 1,000 subjects. Bias and mean squared error compare estimators against each method's own stated target, whose scale/definition can differ. The models omit confounders and use one effect setting and sample size; these simulations do not test causal discovery from arbitrary observational software data.

The ordinary mediator equation is **M on X**, whose coefficient supplies the product`ab`; this is not the reciprocal of an X-on-M fit. The paper distinguishes that product from`c−c′`. In logistic models, adding an outcome predictor changes coefficient scaling even without mediation. Thus conditional/marginal coefficient differences alone can misrepresent an indirect effect. Y-standardization, full-standardization and a residual-variance logistic adjustment are considered, rather than presumed cures.

The reported simulations favor crude`ab`and`ab/(ab+c′)`and the selected potential-outcome estimates relative to their specified targets. For a continuous mediator and prevalence.5, Table 2 has product bias−.001/MSE.002 versus difference bias−.068/MSE.006. Standardization improves several difference-based estimates; it generally does not improve the favored product estimator, and some proportion estimates worsen. Binary-mediator product and potential-outcome estimates have **different targets**, so similarly low bias does not make them the same causal quantity.

One localized arithmetic discrepancy remains in Table 3: at mediator prevalence.3/outcome prevalence.5, the crude difference estimate has bias−.299 and MSE.081. Under the paper's stated definitions, mean squared error must be at least squared mean error, about.0894; rounding at three decimals cannot explain that gap. This is our printed-value check, not a replacement analysis. It does not overturn the separate scale argument or every favorable comparison.

The empirical illustration uses 360 participants from one measurement round of a longitudinal cohort, with a continuous exposure, dichotomized outcome and continuous/binary versions of the same mediator. These are **two formulations of one cohort**, not independent experimental replications. The continuous-mediator methods agree near−.03/12% for indirect effect/proportion; binary-mediator versions differ near−.06/25% versus−.01/7%. These figures illustrate estimand/scale dependence; this reading makes no substantive clinical conclusion.

For causal interpretation the paper explicitly requires no unobserved confounding and the relevant no-interaction assumptions. Its continuous-mediator approximation also invokes a rare outcome, and the discussion warns that odds ratios need not approximate risk ratios at larger prevalences. These assumptions cannot be established by standardizing coefficients or obtaining statistical significance. The apparent empirical success of particular formulas in the twelve simulated conditions is narrower than a universal identification result.
