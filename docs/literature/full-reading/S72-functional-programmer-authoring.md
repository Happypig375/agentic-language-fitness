## Live claims and design

B02/B08/B09/B10 concern type-driven authoring, deliberately elicited compiler feedback, domain evolution and the limits of extrapolating human practice to agents. The paper studies practice and selected hypotheses; it does not compare static versus dynamic languages or evaluate a new tool.

The qualitative phase combines fifteen recruited programmers' think-aloud sessions and interviews with fifteen manually selected livestream clips of approximately one hour. Selection sought varied languages/tasks, not a probability sample. Live sessions had roughly 45 minutes of coding and 45 minutes of interview, with tasks and follow-up changes adapted during theoretical sampling. The first author coded recordings and developed categories in discussion with the second. Reported saturation applies to that grounded theory process, not field completeness or prevalence. Think-aloud effects are explicitly acknowledged. One live participant and four livestreamers used F#; the quantitative phase used Haskell throughout.

Twelve self-identified Haskell programmers with at least two years' experience participated in the quantitative phase. A 90-minute session always presented the workload task first, Hangman second, then drafting and refactoring a key-value store. This is one small fixed sequence, not independent replications over many task families. The text does not establish whether recruitment populations overlap across the qualitative and quantitative phases.

The workload task stops when a participant declares confidence; the first author additionally judged every solution to meet the specification. This is an author assessment, not blinded independent behavioral testing. All combinator participants queried Hoogle or the REPL for type information. Some drafted with pattern matching before translating, including one who translated the whole solution. These are part of the assigned-condition outcomes, not grounds to remove inconvenient observations. Familiarity, lookup and forced-style translation remain plausible mechanisms.

## Direct relation to Nu and D1

Pages 7 and 14 directly document the wildcard/evolution concern: one participant reports a codebase convention banning wildcards, while another describes a new variant silently taking an old catch-all path. Participants also used catch-alls to focus first on successful cases. These are valuable observations of a real tradeoff, not a measured causal advantage for banning wildcards or an estimate of how often such bugs occur.

The paper distinguishes corrective compiler feedback from deliberately requested guidance. Consequently, a compiler invocation that fails can be productive information gathering; failure counts alone cannot represent maintenance quality or wasted work. Participants sometimes used same-typed but semantically wrong functions, reported intentions that types could not encode, and changed type models when expression development exposed problems. The independent behavioral obligations in D1 therefore remain necessary. A requirement to preserve an initial source organization would also need separate justification: changing representation can be legitimate problem solving.

**Unique:** broad claims that explicit matching, type-guided evolution or compiler-as-task-list behavior are new would be incorrect. The narrower agent-maintenance contrast remains unconfirmed and must be distinguished from this human study. **Valuable:** the convention and diagnostic tradeoffs have direct practitioner motivation, but agent effects, prevalence and net benefit are unmeasured. **Scientifically valid:** this paper sharpens outcome, task-family, expertise, style-assignment and conditional-denominator controls; it does not validate D1's source equivalence, temporal oracle or accounting. Experimental holds remain.
