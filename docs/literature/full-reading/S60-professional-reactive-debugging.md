## Reconstruction and limits

The initial material comprises five informal interviews and five volunteered war stories, not ten standardized interviews. Four interviews concern RxJS-related frontend work; one concerns Scala/akka-streams. Four interviews were in Swiss-German and one in English. War-story respondents include an RxJS core contributor, two Angular experts and two other engineers, one of whom is an author. This is useful situated experience, not an independent representative population sample or an estimate of industrial prevalence.

The subsequent observation involves four developers with RxJS experience, using their own devices and preferred environments. Two authored frontend applications each contain two or three bugs. Participants receive setup practice beforehand, then tackle the applications in two consecutive 25-minute blocks while thinking aloud remotely. Tests disclose functional requirements and determine completion; a block also ends at timeout. Understanding the unfamiliar applications consumes part of the allowance. There is no randomized integrated-tool intervention, counterbalanced tool assignment or otherwise equivalent external-tool comparison.

All four participants add trace logs; two also use ordinary debuggers. None uses a specialized external reactive tool during the observed blocks. Table 2 records **one completed first-application attempt and zero completed second-application attempts**: one success among eight participant/application attempts. Those eight attempts are clustered within four people and two authored applications. Timeouts remain outcomes; they cannot be discarded to estimate typical repair speed.

Two participants report ordinarily using `rxjs-spy` but do not use it during observation. One says decomposition with an external tool would have been the next step after the second block timed out; Table 1's “Next step” is an intention, not observed tool use. The paper's majority-awareness statement aggregates its fourteen peers across unlike collection methods. It should not be treated as a population adoption rate.

The authors interpret setup effort and weak environment integration as reasons for avoidance. Section 3.3 explicitly presents this as speculation, and the threats section acknowledges the time limit as another explanation. Its stronger abstract/conclusion wording does not turn the observation into a causal integration result. The proposed integrated tool and its effectiveness evaluation are future work. S43's controlled result remains evidence for its particular tool bundle and tasks, rather than independent validation of S60's proposed explanation.

## Implications and source frontier

**Unique:** ordinary versus reactive debugging and the gap between availability and use are established topics. No firstness follows for a Nu tool or D1 convention. **Valuable:** observing values alone can miss subscription, cancellation and completion events; setup, extraction and reintegration effort can matter alongside capability. The paper motivates those costs but does not estimate Nu's maintenance benefit. **Scientifically valid:** distinguish tool availability, actual use and assigned interventions; include familiarization, integration effort, unsuccessful attempts and task/person dependence. Do not adopt the hypothesis-loop diagram as a uniquely validated account of human debugging. All existing experimental holds remain in force.
