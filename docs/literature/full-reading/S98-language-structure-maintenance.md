## What was compared

The study asks two different questions: whether LADY's structural concepts improve average maintenance compared with C-TIP, and which structural metrics describe or predict maintenance within LADY. The first receives the detailed language comparison; the second is a summary directing readers to the dissertation and workshop paper.

Both languages have Pascal algorithmic modules and strong typing. **The treatment is a bundle.** LADY adds a third description level: distributed teams, concurrent processes within teams, and modules. It provides message channels between teams and monitored shared memory within teams, separate specification/implementation, a library system with type checking, and language-supported parameterization. C-TIP has processes and modules, message communication only, and lacks several of those specification/library facilities. A language effect here cannot be attributed solely to abstraction, static types, information hiding or a single source convention.

There are **twelve pre-existing systems: two requirement families × two languages × three implementations**. The families are time-sharing systems resembling SOLO and hierarchical ZIP-code parcel-control systems. Table II gives mean LADY/C-TIP sizes of 15.2/10.7 KLOC for time sharing and 2.5/1.5 KLOC for process control; pre-test development effort is 257.9/301.3 and 70.6/87.6 staff-hours. These are neither twelve unrelated problem families nor identically sized implementations.

Six graduate research assistants each maintain one time-sharing and one process-control system in the **same** language. After ranking candidates using grades, experience and judged project performance, ranks 1/4/5 receive C-TIP and 2/3/6 receive LADY. Random assignment is **within language** to systems; language assignment itself follows that balancing rule. Two people who helped develop a system cannot maintain that same system. The paper acknowledges subjectivity in selection, greater prior C-TIP familiarity and a two-week system-familiarization period. Matching and random within-language assignment do not remove all between-person/language confounding.

Each system receives fifty tasks: 25 corrective, ten adaptive and fifteen perfective, giving **600 task observations from six people and twelve systems**. Tasks range from fifteen minutes to more than a day. Environmental/requirement changes are identical within a requirement family. The seeded faults differ across systems: identical representative faults producing identical failures were not achievable. Instead, their distribution follows development fault classes, affects corresponding functions and covers structural levels. The classes distinguish data/control/computation faults and single-/multiple-unit effects. The intended faults are those **not detectable by the compiler**. Six hundred tasks are not six hundred independent language assignments.

The supporting environment includes design documentation, separate language compilers/libraries and functional test tools. Fault isolation uses document reading; changes are validated by testing and reading. Task forms record effort, changed modules and reuse, with author/participant validation meetings. Failure detection is excluded on the assumption that structure does not affect it; that assumption is not tested here. Observed new/missed faults are too few for a meaningful quality comparison.

## Positive, null and adverse observations

Table III reports mean staff-hours per system/task:

| Maintenance cause | LADY total / isolation / correction | C-TIP total / isolation / correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Failure | 1.59 / 0.98 / 0.61 | 2.04 / 1.46 / 0.58 |
| Environmental change | 13.62 / 10.56 / 3.06 | 15.93 / 12.68 / 3.25 |
| Requirement change | 8.27 / 6.37 / 1.90 | 11.24 / 8.96 / 2.28 |

The authors report significant isolation reductions of **32.9%, 16.7% and 28.9%**, respectively, but **no significant correction-effort difference** for any cause. Thus the favorable total means do not demonstrate uniformly faster editing or better final behavior. For failures, the time-sharing systems have isolation means 1.33/2.09 hours, while process-control systems have 0.63/0.84. The favorable isolation difference is significant only for the larger time-sharing family in that split. The suggested explanation is more useful hierarchical comprehension for complex systems; that mechanism is not independently manipulated.

Locality also favors LADY at the module level: mean modules changed per task are **1.92 versus 2.47** for time sharing and **1.62 versus 2.46** for process control. The maximum effort share concentrated in one unit is 85%/74% and 70%/69%. Counting LADY's additional team specification units changes the affected-unit means to 2.71/2.47 and 2.08/2.46; the authors therefore compare modules. This choice avoids treating different structural levels as interchangeable, but also means the favorable module count is not a complete accounting of all documentation units changed.

Table VII's per-module correction means are **0.40/0.28** hours for time sharing and **0.28/0.18** for process control. These numerically favor C-TIP and are described as largely language-independent, not significant LADY benefits. They are derived from aggregate effort and changed-module counts, not a separate randomized module-level experiment.

Rework has an explicit contrary result. Table V's all-development-effort rework percentages are **57.83/44.36** for time sharing and **56.91/40.59** for process control, higher for LADY. Restricting to requirements/system/team-design effort gives **50.29/76.83** and **58.40/58.52**. The early-design subset favors LADY significantly for time sharing, while overall rework does not. Testing-phase effort is considered unreliable and excluded; the paper estimates including it would increase the percentages. Do not replace the overall adverse result with the favorable subset or treat either as an all-lifecycle cost estimate.

For requirement changes, about 60% of newly integrated LADY modules are reused unchanged and another 20% need interface changes, compared with about 45% of C-TIP modules reused unchanged or slightly changed. Parameterized module types and libraries are plausible mechanisms. Team reuse is limited because changing ports/module-object counts requires a new team type. No validated metric explains reuse, and broader reuse of designs or experience is not measured. These descriptive reuse proportions are not a demonstrated causal effect of typing alone.

## Inference and metric boundaries

Language comparisons use Mann–Whitney U tests over system classes, rather than declaring every task independent. However, each maintainer contributes two systems, and the paper provides no individual/system distributions, U values, confidence intervals, tail specification or dependence adjustment. It says the best attainable significance level for two groups of three is .05, while several family-specific claims use `p < .05`. For a tie-free exact three-versus-three permutation test, the most extreme allocation has probability 1/20 one-sided and 2/20 two-sided. This arithmetic illustrates why tails and calculation details matter; it does not reconstruct the unpublished data or establish that every reported comparison is wrong. Aggregated six-versus-six comparisons have a different resolution. The fuller-method gap remains explicit.

The metric summary considers internal length/structure/interface-access intensity, external information flows, and combinations. Implicit flows include dependencies not represented by explicit interface/global data, such as a shared buffer-size assumption. Reported average Spearman correlations favor combinations: about .90 for effort, .85 for isolation, .92 for locality and .83 for per-module modification. Removing implicit flows weakens several correlations but enables automated measurement. Simple length/structure performs well for modifiability; it does not explain locality in this account. Reusability has no validated metric.

Those correlations support **association within this study**, not a calibrated prospective prediction rule. The journal summarizes the best metrics after considering many combinations and does not give the complete metric selection process, sample sizes per correlation, uncertainty, held-out prediction results or independent validation. An ordinal correlation does not estimate hours saved by choosing a structure. The author explicitly requires revalidation in a different environment and proposes later work; the paper's positive findings should be retained with those limits.

## Survey consequences and next action

S98 supplies a positive counterweight to S97's inconclusive immediate-process findings: structural/language packages can reduce **isolation** work on selected systems even when correction speed and fault quality are not improved. Their different languages, tasks, participants and endpoints prevent pooling the studies as one homogeneous effect. Nu's multiple architectural conventions similarly need mechanism-specific assumptions and observed outcomes; this paper does not estimate a Nu, F#/C#, coding-agent or D1 effect.

**Unique:** unconfirmed; language-supported organization and maintenance outcomes have substantial predecessors. **Valuable:** concrete isolation/locality/reuse gains coexist with correction nulls, greater overall rework and unmeasured transfer. **Scientifically valid:** complete journal reconstruction, with bundled assignment, six-person dependence, heterogeneous faults and incomplete statistics/metric validation stated. Next, read S154's accessible design-metric method to resolve that dependency while retaining S153's now-identified body/access gap. S90 and S152 remain separate access gaps; the wider B01–B12 survey and all experimental holds remain open.
