## Mechanism and actual evidence

The engine specializes in point-and-click adventures with limited object/action combinations and relatively modest performance demands (pp. 28–29). Objects, scenes, actions, reactions and conversations form a typed description. Reactions transform a `World` and optionally return a pending reaction; an existential wrapper permits heterogeneous reaction chains (pp. 30–32). The architecture therefore directly precedes broad claims of functional game-world transformations or declarative coordination. It does not implement Nu's full general-purpose lifecycle model.

The static description library is separated from the runtime engine, letting the IDE generate definitions independently (p. 33). The paper reports fewer than 450 lines for core definitions, fewer than 2,000 lines across 46 engine modules, and roughly 12,000 lines across 386 IDE modules, excluding comments/module declarations. Template Haskell generates substantial UI and accessor code. These are different scopes, not a matched comparison demonstrating less total development effort, greater robustness or lower maintenance cost.

The evidence consists of architecture, illustrative definitions, screenshots and author-reported implementations. Most game screenshots illustrate the genre or other Haskell games; they are not controlled GALE outcomes. Figure 10 shows reactive rules and a protected model coordinating the IDE's asynchronous compilation thread. No participant study, comparative error rate, workload distribution or performance benchmark is reported.

The explicit limitations on pp. 34–35 matter more than introductory capability language. Animation is step-based; real-time FRP integration remains an extension. Actions are fixed even though reactions are extensible. The engine cannot yet save and resume game state. The IDE has no testing facilities; recorded replay and temporal assertions remain future work. Some static checks exist, but broader analysis is proposed. Repeated reaction definitions and the lack of IDE copy/paste are practical costs. Figure 9's broad target-platform caption must be read with the prose's incomplete IDE packaging support. A pure world representation does not by itself supply persistence, replay, behavioral coverage or a complete deployment workflow.

## Consequences and follow-up

**Unique:** unconfirmed for a precise Nu mechanism; pure game-world transformations and functional game tooling have direct prior art. Avoid a general firstness claim. **Valuable:** composition, analysis and cross-platform reuse are plausible mechanisms, accompanied by expressiveness and tooling costs; comparative maintenance benefit remains unmeasured. **Scientifically valid:** a later evaluation needs matched supported obligations, an explicit engine/tool/generated-code boundary, realistic lifecycle transitions and independent behavioral oracles. LOC and a demonstration cannot substitute for complete-outcome evidence. ALF's validation and allocation remain unchanged at zero experiments.

References 5 and 8 are the already promoted S48 time-travel and S46 testing papers. Their concrete replay semantics remain consequential, rather than importing GALE's future-work promises. Earlier FRP foundations, monadic-stream-function modularity, the reactive IDE abstraction and the documental adventure approach remain conditional on adopting their specific guarantees or a genre-specific comparator.
