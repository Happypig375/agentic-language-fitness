## Decision and evidence class

This closes a material B01/B06/B09/B11/B12 foundation gap: what does the classic coupling/cohesion argument actually propose, and which claims about easier evolution are mechanisms, heuristics or measured effects? A rival to any architecture-specific benefit is that a chosen decomposition happens to fit the selected changes, independently of language, source size or runtime facilities. Different future changes, reuse contexts or call overhead could change the preferred decomposition.

The authors propose a design method drawing on experience, with explanatory examples and a worked monitoring design. They explicitly omit the theory's derivation. The first reference/footnote says the method had **not undergone a formal IBM test** and advises evaluating usefulness in the intended environment. There is no sampled maintenance experiment, randomized assignment, measured person-time, quantitative error reduction or uncertainty estimate in this paper. Strong claims in its conclusion retain the status of design rationale and practitioner judgment. That status does not erase the concrete mechanisms it explains.

Here, a module is a named, callable, contiguous set of statements, preferably with distinct variable names, such as a procedure or subroutine. The target is the programmer's ability to understand and change the source. This differs from [S89's responsibility-based module criterion](S89-information-hiding-decomposition.md), which need not align with a procedure, runtime object or compilation unit. Neither definition can be silently equated with an arbitrary package/class metric or model context window.

## Coupling is more than a count of edges

Table 1 distinguishes interface complexity/obscurity, references to the public module versus its internals, and data versus control versus code-modifying communication. The authors expressly say that these interacting factors make a simple coupling index difficult. Their connection is a reference to a label/address defined elsewhere, not a modern universal dependency metric.

Shared storage can make unrelated modules sensitive to the same representation or updates. The expression **N × M × (M − 1)** counts potential directed first-order relationships for N shared elements and M modules: the paper's N=25, M=3 example gives 150. This is combinatorial accounting under its common-environment model, not an estimated number of defects, actual edits or cognitive operations. Narrower shared groups reduce the modeled exposure; language layout, access restrictions and actual usage affect transfer to another setting.

The terminal example in Figures 2–3 makes the mechanism concrete. A reader gets its terminal address from another module's internal/global variable. A new caller can temporarily change that value, but an interleaving call can then read from the wrong terminal even if the value is eventually restored. Passing the address explicitly permits each caller to specify its context. This illustrates interference through hidden shared state; passing an argument alone does not prove purity, eliminate aliasing or validate all concurrent behavior.

Figures 4–5 replace a parsed/unparsed selector with separate operations that share the unparsed-reading subfunction. The proposal removes a flag and its internal test while exposing the distinct requested functions. It is an interface example, not measured superiority of every split API. The paper later permits error/EOF result flags, distinguishes reporting a condition from telling the caller what action to take, and explicitly presents transaction dispatch as a useful structure. It therefore does not establish that all branching, domain tags or catch-all cases are poor design. D1's explicit enumeration versus equivalent catch-all remains a separate proposed comparison.

## Binding, duplication and the expected change

This edition lists **six** cohesion/binding categories, in the stated order: coincidental, logical, temporal, communicational, sequential and functional. It calls the scale nonlinear. Later seven-category formulations or numerical object-oriented metrics should not be attributed to this text without their own sources. The highest applicable relationship classifies an element pair; pairs lacking functional binding lower the module's binding.

The examples distinguish coincidental repeated statements from one reusable function. A shared block that happens to combine arithmetic, card input and output becomes awkward when just one caller needs tape input. Sharing the whole block couples that new requirement to other callers. The positive recommendation is to consolidate a function that should change together, while retaining separate copies of incidental repetition when their future responsibilities differ. Shorter source is not the criterion by itself.

Temporal grouping, such as initialization, can be simpler than selecting among unrelated operations but is weaker than a common goal under this classification. Sequential data flow is also distinguished from functional cohesion: adjacent steps can contain several functions or only part of one. A short purpose sentence is offered as a heuristic for finding a single goal, with ambiguity acknowledged. The authors admit that how far to subdivide remains unresolved. One/two-page guidance, scrutiny below five or above 100 executable statements, and a cited roughly thirty-statement comprehension claim are recommendations or external claims, not calibrated results of this paper. The cited comprehension method was not read here.

A useful change example separates dialing from receiving rather than making receiving a necessary part of dialing. A later need to transmit can reuse the dialer without a new switch or duplicate dialer. The locality claim is conditional on the problem's responsibilities and changes aligning with the decomposition. S89 instead foregrounds difficult/likely-to-change design decisions, but S93 also recommends isolating record-layout, data-type and indexing dependencies. These approaches overlap; a caricature of S93 as merely chopping a flowchart would miss its explicit criticism of that practice.

## Predictability, observability and resource tradeoffs

A module can be individually reliable yet context-dependent. The alternating zero/one oscillator works as implemented, but two callers can interfere unless their call protocol is coordinated. The paper favors behavior determined by explicit inputs and limited environmental dependence, while recognizing I/O functions and initialization/error handling. This is prior reasoning about predictable interfaces and state interaction, not a purity type system, persistent-state runtime or state-migration proof.

Observability appears in the opening design criteria; clear names, interfaces and comments support use without internal knowledge. Later entry/return tracing and stubs are proposed debugging/testing aids. These ideas precede Nu, while providing no automatic oracle for retained behavior, scheduling, external effects or all future inputs. Its own monitoring example abstracts away many detailed runtime obligations.

The paper explicitly balances maintenance/debugging against procedure-call time and memory. It proposes optional code loading, flexible overlay/virtual-storage grouping, shared functions and fewer selectors as possible savings. It also proposes replacing hot calls with branches or inline mechanisms after profiling, acknowledging the extra naming/interface obligations this can introduce. These are workload-, language- and implementation-dependent possibilities, with no measured speed/memory result. Logical modularity and the physical implementation of a call must remain distinct, as in S89 and the later reconstructed runtime studies.

## The actual construction procedure and worked example

Figures 8–13 give a five-step input/transform/output method: sketch the functional problem; identify conceptual streams independently of physical devices; locate the most abstract incoming/outgoing forms; define source, transformation and sink interfaces; recursively expand the source/sink transformations. Finding those boundaries is explicitly subjective, and no detailed rule is provided for decomposing the central transform. Designers are advised to inspect the whole structure together to avoid incompatible local decisions. These are method steps, not a proven automatic algorithm or new construction authorization for this project.

A structure chart records call responsibility and parameter flow. A flowchart records execution sequence/conditions; Figure 7 demonstrates that their orders can differ. Hiding a decision from the general-design diagram deliberately postpones detail, rather than proving the decision or behavior irrelevant. The transaction structure in Figure 9 handles different commands/events with different actions and can occur within the broader transformation structure.

Figures 16–22 work through a hypothetical patient-monitoring program with periodic readings, stored observations, range checks, failed-device handling and notification. No deployed clinical system or measured safety result is reported. A failed terminal read affects both notification and selection of another patient. Initially those affected modules lie outside the deciding module's subordinate tree. The redesign merges the low-level read into a higher routine and moves patient selection beneath it, aligning **scope of effect** with **scope of control**. This is a concrete structural response to a scattered decision, with the callable responsibilities changed accordingly. It neither proves semantic equivalence nor specifies fairness, retry bounds, timing, notification delivery or state consistency for all executions.

The useful general lesson is to identify which behavior a decision controls and where it is coordinated. The lesson is not that every temporal obligation can be reduced to a call tree. The later S162/S186 operational cases remain empirical/practitioner evidence of obligations outside locally successful code and tests; S93 supplies a design rationale at a different evidential level.

## Consequence and continuation

For characterizing Nu, weak coupling, functional grouping, predictable interfaces, observation aids, input/transform/output separation and conditional change locality are established predecessor ideas. A defensible Nu contribution could still concern a particular mechanism, enforced property, integration or demonstrated benefit; that requires a narrower source-grounded comparison. The 1974 argument alone does not establish a functional-language, immutable-world, agent-maintenance or compactness advantage, nor negate later measured benefits. S91's metric associations, S92/S94's change tasks and S96's professional case retain their separate positive/null/adverse scopes.
