Live B01/B10/B12 question: do commonly optimized structural properties predict the professional judgment they are meant to improve, and is that judgment itself evidence of maintenance benefit?

## Designs, metrics and participants

Five experienced engineers produced designs for each of two standard educational problems: an ATM and a cruise-booking system. The researchers made minor adjustments to ten UML class models for consistent abstraction and a range of metric values. No available refactoring tool met their requirements, so **the rated models were not generated by the search tools being criticized**. They are neither ten randomly sampled industrial systems nor observed before/after refactorings.

Participants receive one randomly assigned problem/model and rate understandability, reusability and flexibility on seven-point agreement scales. Definitions adapted from QMOOD are available while answering. The study omits QMOOD's three other quality attributes to reduce burden. Qualitative explanations, experience, self-assessed expertise and confidence in ratings accompany the judgments.

A review within the SBSE repository starts with 57 “software metrics” search results and selects 23 papers, reporting 118 different metrics. This is the authors' bounded selection, not a comprehensive current metric census. Frequently used LCOM, MQ and EVM measures are considered but not tested because they require implementation/dependency information outside these class models.

The five tested measures are design size in classes (DSC), direct class coupling (DCC), number of methods (NOM), numbers among classes (NAC), and number of attributes and methods (NOAM). The first three come from QMOOD, NAC from earlier work, and NOAM is added to cover attribute counts. Inheritance measures are excluded because only six models have a single hierarchy. Exact per-model values and detailed computation conventions are delegated to online materials rather than fully specified in the paper.

This tests five individual structural metrics. It does not evaluate the whole QMOOD quality formula, all 118 metrics, LCOM, source-level data/control flow, every weighted combination or a deployed search optimizer. The authors explicitly recognize that trade-offs in a weighted composite can make marginal correlations insufficient.

The survey runs for 42 days, January 18–February 28, 2015. Invitations go to roughly 900 ACCU members and 200 Bristol BCS members; a LinkedIn BCS forum has around 11,000 potential viewers, with actual exposure unknown. Overlap, viewing, participation and incomplete responses are not reported sufficiently to calculate a valid overall response rate.

Fifty responses are reported; the paper says one was discarded and refers to section 8, but that section does not identify an exclusion rule or case. Thus **49 intended retained responses** should not be substituted uncritically for every displayed denominator. Figure 2 reports 76% positive self-assessed expertise, 14% neutral and 10% negative. For confidence in the supplied-model ratings, only 16% are positive, 34% neutral and 50% negative. These describe different questions. Experience and self-rated expertise do not establish that participants had adequate information or confidence for this particular judgment.

Five experienced engineers pretest the questionnaire. Their relationship to the five design authors and the final respondent population is not specified sufficiently to claim independent cohorts. Recall feedback leads to more explicit back buttons; later mobile-device failures may still affect participation. Public forum criticism of diagram-only judgment also creates an unmeasured potential influence on who responds and how.

## Quantitative result

The study computes Spearman correlations between individual ratings and the assigned model's metric values. The printed coefficients and two-sided p-values are:

| Rated quality | DSC | DCC | NOM | NAC | NOAM |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Understandable | −.128 (.375) | −.271 (.0571) | −.203 (.156) | −.0400 (.783) | .103 (.478) |
| Reusable | −.0280 (.847) | −.158 (.272) | −.195 (.174) | −.200 (.163) | .0572 (.693) |
| Flexible | .0386 (.790) | −.0806 (.578) | −.0677 (.640) | −.0613 (.672) | .202 (.160) |

None of the fifteen tests reaches .05. Absolute correlations range from .028 to .271 in this selected sample. That is useful descriptive evidence that these individual metrics do not strongly track these particular ratings. It is **not proof of zero association**, a formal equivalence test, a validated failure of all composite metrics, or evidence that metric-guided changes cannot help.

Participants share ten designs and two domains, repeating the same metric values; a larger respondent count does not create more independent design instances. There are three ratings per respondent, ties in scales/metrics and possible design/domain heterogeneity. The paper does not supply per-model response counts, the exact retained analysis table, uncertainty intervals, hierarchical treatment or sensitivity to the discarded response. The lost supplement prevents reconstructing those details. No new statistical estimate is manufactured from scatter-plot dots or rounded coefficients.

Random model presentation addresses assignment within this elicitation. It does not randomize an intervention that changes a metric while holding other design properties fixed. The analysis is correlational and its outcome is perceived quality, not executed comprehension, successful maintenance, regression avoidance or effort.

## Qualitative result and positive contribution

Two authors independently code prose responses, then resolve differences through discussion. Responses may receive several codes; frequency totals therefore exceed the participant count. The tables do not provide a pre-resolution agreement statistic, a released codebook or full response-to-code mapping.

For understandability, 22 coded responses ask for additional dynamics/context/requirements/rationale; thirteen mention problematic responsibility assignment, ten traceability to the domain, six separation of purpose and five naming. For flexibility, twelve discuss modifiability of parts, eleven problem specificity, eight additional information and eight missing/incorrect abstractions. For reusability, 24 mention problem specificity and eighteen distinguish reusable from nonreusable parts.

These are informative descriptions of the conditions practitioners want to examine. They are not mutually exclusive votes, proportions of all professional developers, measured causal contributions to quality or calibrated demonstrations that an expert chose the better system. Structural concerns still appear: coupling, responsibility, abstractions and separation of concerns. The study's positive contribution is to make missing domain/behavioral information and intended reuse/change context explicit, rather than to show that structural information has no use.

The authors recommend behavior information and human involvement in search-based refactoring. Those proposals were **not evaluated interventions** in this survey. The claim that human involvement is the only viable route goes beyond the finite metric/rating result; automated semantic, behavioral, contextual or combined methods were not compared. Nor does the study establish that adding a statechart would improve correctness or reduce effort—its own discussion treats that as future work.

## Synthesis and continuation

Together with [S158](S158-practitioner-design-quality.md) and [S160](S160-belief-and-evidence.md), S161 supports separating a practitioner's reported reasoning, a structural proxy and observed outcomes. It adds specific evidence of low confidence in judgments from limited diagrams. It does not establish human ratings as a maintenance oracle.

For Nu/ISE, semantic and temporal obligations, domain purpose and integration context can matter even when structural metrics are easy to compute. This is a reason to define and check independent behavior and actual effort; it is not evidence that Nu's organization is better, that small source is always better, or that human review guarantees success. D1's practical benefit and priority remain unconfirmed.
