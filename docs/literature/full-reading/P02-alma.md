## Problem, definitions and method

ALMA distinguishes modifiability (adaptation to environment, requirements and functional specification) from broader maintainability including bug correction (p. 130, section 2.1). Its five stages are goal, architecture description, scenario elicitation, impact evaluation and interpretation; these iterate rather than form a rigid waterfall (p. 133, section 4). It offers three distinct goals: expected maintenance effort, risk discovery and comparison of architectural alternatives. A single preferred source metric cannot stand in for all three.

Description includes component responsibilities, direct/indirect dependencies and relationships to the environment. Impact analysis identifies affected components, their changes and recursive ripple effects; missing detail must be recorded or obtained from the architect, not assumed away (pp. 133-135, sections 4.2-4.4). Architectural views underdetermine implementation-level effects. The paper cites earlier impact-prediction limitations; those cited studies were not independently read in this pass.

Scenario selection follows the decision: likely changes for effort estimation, complex/adverse changes for risk, and common discriminating scenarios for architecture comparison. Elicitation can start from categories or from stakeholder examples; it iterates until categories have been considered and new examples no longer change the classification (p. 134, section 4.3). This is a method-specific stopping criterion, not proof of an exhaustive universe or a warrant to stop literature discovery. Risk classification also depends on owner priorities and likelihood (pp. 141-142).

## Evidence-generating cases and contrasts

The article explains three cases and cites seven action-research applications plus a prior scenario-elicitation experiment as its empirical foundation (pp. 145-146, section 8). Researchers participated in the projects; these are not randomized trials or seven independent estimates of prediction accuracy.

* MPC: four stakeholder roles elicited 32 scenarios, subsequently categorized/revised; the product manager supplied expected occurrence weights. Architecture/component-size estimates and estimates of modified/new code feed an effort model (pp. 136-138, section 5). The paper publishes only two scenario rows and assumes productivity values from other literature, explicitly advising organization-specific calibration.
* EASY: three stakeholders, interviews about 1-2 hours, 21 scenarios. The instrument records initiator, internal/external impact, multiple owners and version conflicts; two scenarios are ultimately classified as risks. A middleware change is complex but considered low-probability, so complexity alone does not make it a prioritized risk (pp. 139-142, section 6, tables 2-4).
* Beer-can inspection: five common change scenarios assess six design/analysis states (initial, iterations 1-4, final). Assessors also designed the system and used informal knowledge. The outcome is the predicted fraction of components modified, not observed agent success or realized change cost (pp. 143-145, section 7, table 5). No candidate model, tool/memory policy, runtime test oracle or inferential confidence interval exists in this conceptual/action-research study.

## Results, arithmetic and limits

Table 5 gives initial-to-final fractions: S1 1/4 to 2/10; S2 4/4 to 2/10; S3 4/4 to 2/10; S4 4/4 to 3/10; S5 4/4 to 10/10. Thus four fractions improve and one is unchanged; S1 improves fractionally while the absolute changed-component count increases from one to two. This directly cautions against treating a lower normalized structural metric as less actual coordination. Iteration 4 worsens S1/S2 while improving S3 (p. 145, figure 7). The prose's statement that two scenarios remain on the same level does not describe the initial-to-final table; retain the table's exact values rather than silently harmonize it.

For the two disclosed MPC rows, `0.0345*5000 + 0.0023*4750 = 183.425` modified LOC and `0.0345*5000 = 172.5` new LOC, matching the approximate 183/173 printed values (p. 137, table 1). These are partial weighted contributions, not the full confidential prediction. Equation 1 normalizes scenario weights to sum to one. Equation 2 then divides a weighted sum by scenario count and multiplies by productivity, while the surrounding text quotes productivity in LOC/month (p. 138). The intended rate inversion and normalization conventions are not sufficiently clear for direct implementation: multiplying LOC by LOC/month is not person-months, and a second division of a normalized expectation changes its scale. Do not import this expression as ALF's cost estimator or silently repair the source.

Most consequentially, the authors explicitly say they lack firm quantitative data establishing prediction accuracy or risk coverage (p. 146, section 8). Published adoption of ALMA therefore does not validate ALF's prediction cards. Scenario framing and useful description are reusable; prospective predictive performance still has to be tested against independent observed outcomes.

## Decision for ALF

Retain common change demands, recorded responsibility/ripple predictions and adverse scenarios as an **adaptation of ALMA**, with the actual decision and analyst involvement explicit. Separate realistic-frequency and stress-selection claims; equal chain weights do not estimate field prevalence. Keep ties in the evidence, including useful failures of discrimination, even though ALMA permits excluding ties when choosing a winner. Freeze predictions before witnesses, preserve revisions, and score later behavioral obligations separately from source-size/component ratios. No randomly sampled architects or prior Nu approval are inferred as prerequisites; absence of an independent expert must nevertheless be visible. All construction remains subject to the later human decision.
