## Live question and mechanism

B05/B07/B10/B11/B12 need a concrete account of the cost and validity of observing an interactive system. S159 studies coverage collection in Ubisoft's industrial C++ setting, using a custom LLVM 18 fork. It supplies positive feasibility and test-stability results under specified instrumentation scopes. It does not establish that executed code satisfies its behavioral obligations, that unchanged code cannot regress or that coverage feedback improves maintenance outcomes.

The mechanism combines established compiler instrumentation with a commit-derived selection and existing build/test infrastructure. A selective instrumentation context (SIC) is a subset of functions. The selected context is normally the functions added or modified by a commit, applied over an approved baseline. Developers request coverage when triggering an isolated build. A .NET orchestration API generates a profile list, extends the distributed build, collects profiles via a game-wrapper exit hook, processes them with LLVM tools and links coverage to a build/test report.

The pipeline retrieves added/modified **`.cpp` files**, intersects lightweight-parser function spans with diff regions, and targets every function in a newly added file. It recreates ASTs using compilation commands, includes, flags and macro definitions through CppAST.NET, extended to extract Clang mangled names. Matching the target function name alone can include unchanged overloads; the authors acknowledge this reduction in selection precision. Compilation uses a profile list with selected mangled names allowed and instrumentation otherwise disabled. The paper does not validate coverage of header-only changes, deletions, changed macros or behavioral effects in untouched functions under this stated rule. Those are unresolved correspondence questions, not reproduced implementation failures.

Frontend (FE) instrumentation retains source mapping and supports line-level coverage in the described configuration. IR instrumentation is later in the optimization pipeline and supplies cheaper, less granular function/frequency information. The paper compares both against no instrumentation and full instrumentation. It also acknowledges prior manual filtering and compiler-assisted selection in HPC. Versioned [Clang 18.1.8 documentation](https://releases.llvm.org/18.1.8/tools/clang/docs/UsersManual.html#instrumenting-only-selected-files-or-functions) explicitly describes `-fprofile-list` and patch-modified file/function selection; the industrial contribution is not invention of selective instrumentation itself. The authors' custom fork and integration have not been source-verified.

The wrapper relies on exit-time output. [Versioned coverage documentation](https://releases.llvm.org/18.1.8/tools/clang/docs/SourceBasedCodeCoverage.html#running-the-instrumented-program) distinguishes ordinary exit-time profiles from continuous recording, with platform-specific limits. S159 does not demonstrate recovery of profiles from every crash, timeout or forced termination. Recording and upload completeness remain separate from the granularity of counters that were successfully collected.

## What was measured

Experiments 1 and 2 use **one unnamed AAA title**, with more than a million functions and the latest 100 commits. Experiment 3 uses two titles of different genres, with separate teams' suites but the same testing framework. These are not 100 independent projects or two independent replications of every cost experiment.

| Quantity | Definition and comparison | Actual interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instrumented Function Ratio, IFR | Selected functions divided by all functions in the compilation path | Selection size, not execution coverage, test adequacy or proportion of requirements checked. |
| Profile Extraction Ratio, PER | Selection-extraction wall time divided by baseline compilation wall time | Additional latency before compilation; baseline is the mean of five non-instrumented compilations. Full build asset/download stages are excluded. |
| Compilation CPU ratio | Total CPU time across workers divided by mean baseline CPU time from five compilations | Compute cost, deliberately distinct from elapsed time under scheduling/contention. It cannot be substituted directly for developer waiting time. |
| FPS ratio | Average sampled test FPS divided by average baseline FPS | Relative performance in the selected scenario script; no per-frame latency distribution or worst-frame guarantee. |

Table 1 gives median-commit IFR **2.78 × 10⁻⁶**, largest-commit **5.56 × 10⁻⁴**, union of 100 commits **1.11 × 10⁻³**, and an artificial high-frequency selection with the largest-commit IFR. The high-frequency set is chosen using a profile of the same performance test. It stresses known hot functions; it is not a proof of the worst possible workload, change or hardware behavior. No overlap with the 100-commit union supports a local observation, not a population frequency estimate.

## Positive results and their cost boundaries

**Selection extraction:** Figure 4 reports a median PER of **17.56%**, minimum 11.31%, and maximum **183.61%**; the prose prints **183.32%** for the maximum. The outlying refactoring touches 98 files and 799 functions. Per-file normalization ranges from 0.75% to 25.59%, median 8.33%. Thus favorable low compilation-counter overhead does not make selection extraction free, and normalizing by changed files is not a validated prediction model. The small printed maximum discrepancy remains unresolved without data.

**Compilation:** the chosen commit selections have CPU ratios near baseline. Large explicit lists up to IFR 0.5 produce steep fitted slopes, **128.08 FE / 139.84 IR**, and roughly 65–70 times baseline CPU at the largest selection shown. Fully instrumenting without a selection list is much cheaper to compile: reported ratios **1.75 FE / 1.00 IR**. The authors hypothesize replicated list storage/lookups as the explanation; no independent profiling or source audit confirms it here. Figure 5 reports a least-squares fit and a 95% confidence band, without released raw values or a reconstructible analysis recipe.

The **2,000-commit** claim is an extrapolation from average changed-function counts and a roughly 1% IFR CPU budget, not an observed 2,000-commit build. Selection unions, overlapping edits, future commit sizes and profile extraction all matter. Preserve its application-specific estimate, but do not report it as an end-to-end latency guarantee or evidence that compilation scales uniformly with more instrumentation. The distinction between explicit-list selection and full instrumentation is consequential.

**Runtime:** four selected contexts and FE/IR builds are evaluated using five performance-test runs per build on the same hardware. The wording about builds does not provide a fully reconstructible independent build-replicate count. An automated scenario sequence exercises rendering, physics, assets and AI. The paper reports high average FPS ratios for actual median/largest/batched commits, with much worse performance for the constructed hot-function set. Full instrumentation has reported median FPS ratios **0.297 FE / 0.369 IR**.

Figure 6 visibly places the hot-function IR box below 0.5 and includes a batch outlier below 0.9. Therefore the abstract/conclusion's **above-50%** statement and the body’s claim that actual-commit results consistently exceed 0.9 cannot be treated as universal bounds over the plotted observations. This is a visual discrepancy, not a reconstructed exact raw value. The favorable central tendency for ordinary selected commits remains. Sampled average FPS cannot characterize stutter, rare stalls or timing correctness; the authors explicitly acknowledge the missing frame-time traces.

## Test stability, the useful rival and missing adequacy evidence

Game A has **31 tests**, Game B **44**, targeting AI and 3Cs functionality. All pass in the uninstrumented baseline on the same hardware. Full FE instrumentation yields **six failures in A and 23 in B**. **Full IR has zero reported failures**, as do FE and IR under the selected hot-function context. The stability experiment does not report a repeat count or failure-rate uncertainty. Passing these fixed suites is not an equivalence proof across behaviors, workloads or machines.

The authors inspect three failure modes under full FE:

- Game B initialization/world-loading timeouts; tests with overridden longer timeouts can pass. Default timeouts were removed in a follow-up to inspect later failures, which is a changed diagnostic condition, not proof that the original tests passed.
- Delayed loading or behavior transitions violating timing-sensitive assertions, including NPC interactions.
- Slower physics-driven behavior missing an expected state within a fixed number of frames, illustrated by failure to reach 3.5 m/s in a movement test.

These make observation-induced failures concrete. They do not show that disabling timing assertions or extending every timeout is safe: the original obligations, observed execution conditions and diagnostic intervention must remain distinct. Baseline success and common hardware support the instrumentation comparison, while the private setup, unstated repeat/randomization details and unavailable traces limit attribution to a precise internal mechanism.

**Full IR is a real competing result**, not an omitted baseline: it also preserves the observed suite outcomes, while sacrificing source-line detail. Selective FE is useful when that detail is worth the selection/integration cost. Higher average FPS and preserved suite passes are different outcomes; neither alone establishes useful coverage or fault sensitivity.

The paper acknowledges that changed-function selection can omit relevant execution paths and proposes future AST/call-graph expansion. It measures neither lost fault detections nor completeness against all relevant changed behaviors, and supplies no human debugging-time, repaired-defect, coverage-use or net maintenance-benefit endpoint. An unobserved statement is not demonstrated unreachable code. B07 must retain independent old/new behavioral obligations, temporal triggers, pending work, external effects and capture failures alongside any coverage map.
