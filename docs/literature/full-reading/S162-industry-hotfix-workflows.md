## Study and evidence units

The paper reuses the surveys from [*Behind the Hot Fix* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3696630.3728564): **24 Zühlke respondents plus 136 public respondents**, adding **nine interviews**, one senior practitioner per selected Zühlke project. The journal is not a second independent survey. Individual overlap between the original company survey and interviews is not established. The cases also contain retrospective accounts of earlier employment; nine project-centered interviews are not nine prospectively monitored interventions.

The anonymous Microsoft Forms instrument has 38 branched questions, at most 28 presented to a participant, estimated at ten minutes. It asks about profile, terminology, practice and automation. A professional-employment eligibility branch and an attention item are included. Two uninvolved coauthors and a research-group pilot refine the instrument; pilot responses are excluded. Public recruitment uses the authors' networks, social media, email, referrals and conference promotion, with responses dated February 16–May 26, 2024. Three of 139 public respondents are excluded for never working in a professional software company; all retained respondents pass the attention check. No incentive or sampling-frame response rate is reported. Diversity and seniority make accounts informative without making this a probability sample of companies or incidents.

After initial terminology questions, the survey defines hot fixing as an improvement deployed to address a time-critical production issue; the interview definition additionally says unplanned. **Urgency/mitigation and runtime replacement are different dimensions.** A hotfix can be a release, rollback, configuration change, temporary containment or root-cause change. Live patching is one possible deployment technique, not a condition for inclusion here.

One author codes the survey's three open-text questions and another reviews codes/themes, suggesting two naming changes. Nine interviewees are purposively recruited through two company coauthors for operational visibility and varied domains, roles and settings. Each roughly thirty-minute interview follows a flexible four-part guide, with a separate profile form. Zoom transcripts are checked against recordings. Case summaries preserve individual settings before cross-case coding and synthesis. Reported saturation after the finite set is coded does not establish that unrepresented sectors or roles would add no themes. Raw coded data are withheld for ethical/confidentiality reasons; the published cases and categories, rather than transcripts, are the inspectable basis.

## Survey findings retained at their measured scope

| Question | Reported or printed result | What the result measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical elapsed deployment time | Zühlke: 14/24 choose a few hours; public: 51/136 choose a few days and 46/136 a few hours | Retrospective coarse response categories, not logged incident durations, a mean time-to-repair or a causal company comparison |
| People involved | 67/136 report two to four; 37/136 one; 26/136 unsure/not applicable | Respondents' usual-work estimates; no measured staffing/productivity effect |
| Discovery | 56.7% users, 16.9% crashes, 19.1% both | Selected categories; user reports and monitoring are complementary, not exclusive proof of complete fault coverage |
| Monthly frequency | One-to-five is the largest observed/contributed category, 59/136 and 61/136 | Company-level estimates with 26/35 not-applicable responses; no denominator of deployments, ordinary bugs or service-years |
| Automation, probably/definitely present | Detection 44/136; creation 34/136; verification 73/136; deployment 77/136 | Respondents' knowledge of tooling, not tool inventories, effectiveness or deployment-wide prevalence |

The automation ordering is a useful positive description: later stages are more often reported as automated than detection/generation. The absence of known automation does not establish the absence of suitable methods. The paper itself distinguishes current APR techniques from unmeasured suitability under hotfix constraints. Its recommendation to emphasize generation research is an inference from reported tooling gaps, while interviews also prioritize diagnosis, observability and selective validation. Neither priority is experimentally compared.

The aggregated process has eight stages: discovery, reporting, triage, remediation, testing, deployment, post-deployment verification and post-mortem. **No single survey response lists all eight.** This is a descriptive synthesis of partial accounts, not a validated universal pipeline or evidence that every incident completes every stage. About 54.5% supply detailed pipeline accounts; three report no single known process. Activities may be compressed, bypassed or deferred. The distinction between testing before deployment and checking behavior afterward is consequential for interactive software whose environmental obligations exceed unit-level code behavior.

## Nine cases and their concrete tradeoffs

| Case | Reported practice / constraint | Consequence retained for the survey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1, mobile | Independent maintenance/forced-upgrade controls, subsystem disabling and backend-first mitigation avoid client release delays. Rare highest-priority app releases reportedly take three to five hours with expedited review. | Preplanned containment can help when delivery is externally constrained; no measured comparison establishes net benefit or safe state preservation. |
| C2, pharmaceutical CMS plus past systems | Current client prioritizes data integrity and halts use on instability. Earlier systems contrast manual deployment with later canaries/automation; one account time-boxes a forward fix before reverting. | Availability and integrity can justify different responses. Retrospective project/process bundles do not isolate an automation effect. |
| C3, machinery | A legacy system coordinates about fifty machines. Reported release capability improves from annual releases/months of verification to weekly releases and an hour when needed, with one or two follow-up fixes often required. Some validation still warrants one-to-three-day downtime. | Physical behavior escapes simulation. A reported fix loses a week's production data yet avoids a more costly factory shutdown: operational restoration and data correctness are distinct outcomes. |
| C4, platform/DevOps | Reports deployments as fast as fifteen minutes; a supposedly trivial direct-production configuration change worsens the issue and must be reverted/reworked. Proposes configurable validation paths. | Fast delivery and an adverse bypass incident coexist. Reduced checks require context-specific justification; no safe-selection rule is validated. |
| C5, cloud microservices | Integrated monitoring and fast deployment, selective omission of static/integration checks, real-time second-developer review, tactical symptom fixes. | Human review is reported as a compensating practice, not proven equivalent to the omitted tests. Availability obligations remain with independent components. |
| C6, live sports | Rollback itself requires reverting code and traversing development/staging/production, reportedly up to two hours. Traffic/configuration failures can require a forward fix instead. | Reversion is not necessarily immediate or sufficient; environmental triggers and recovery latency matter. |
| C7, modular cloud platform | About two dozen independently deployable services, rapid releases and temporary flags. Removal of a messaging component breaks a support workflow for days until users report it. | Local deployability can aid recovery while cross-service behavior remains unobserved. Contract and end-to-end obligations survive modular boundaries. |
| C8, insurance | Emphasizes prevention, reproducible environments, local build/test capability and integrated ownership; hotfixes are rare and treated as process failures. | Prevention-oriented and rapid-recovery approaches are both represented; their lifetime cost/risk is not compared. |
| C9, serverless health research | Moving an entry-point file leaves application tests green but breaks deployment routing, causing endpoint 404s and unusable tablets. Users expose the problem; accumulated releases make rollback unattractive. | A source-preserving refactor can violate infrastructure contracts. Post-deployment endpoint checks were missing; planned checks are not an already validated remedy. |

These accounts make alternatives and costs concrete: flags/containment, rollbacks, forward fixes, staged releases, client updates, accepted downtime, test selection and operational coordination. Positive reports of rapid recovery remain useful practitioner evidence. They are not timed controlled trials or proof that one architecture, language, CI policy or staffing model caused the improvement.

## What the analysis does and does not establish

The seven reported chi-square tests compare category frequencies with **a uniform null**, despite earlier wording about subgroup differences. The released code confirms this. Significant concentration in selected answers does not test whether company size, domain or role explains differences, nor establish an industry standard. Changing the category set changes the null: the contribution statistic excludes an unobserved category, matching the released `value_counts` behavior. The artifact note reproduces three other printed statistics and identifies the unresolved 139-versus-136 demographic chart denominator.

Table 2 maps four overlapping contextual factors to reported cases: domain regulation (four interviewees), architecture (five), customer risk tolerance (three), and team maturity (four). These are coded mentions in nine selected interviews, not prevalence estimates or causal effects. Qualitative accounts plausibly explain heterogeneity, but without linked respondent-level observations or telemetry they do not prove that survey differences are entirely contextual rather than reporting noise. The paper's limitations appropriately acknowledge recall, unavailable operational logs and shared company culture.

The account says modern enterprises have largely moved away from live patching, citing runtime-weaving and monkey-patching sources in the surrounding discussion. This study has no historical adoption series or defined live-patching prevalence denominator. Its operational survey cannot support that broad trend. Preserve the separate runtime-update mechanisms already reconstructed in B02/B04 instead of replacing them with an unsupported adoption verdict.

Reported rapid-release benefits, costly failures and cautious alternatives should remain together. Interview recommendations for modularity, dual/configurable pipelines, pair review, better instrumentation and institutional memory are proposed supports. The paper does not quantify their cost, test their comparative safety, or measure repair-tool performance. Conference/journal data reuse, respondent/company/incident units and reported versus observed behavior remain separate.

## Consequence and continuation

For Nu/ISE, software evolution has obligations beyond generating a locally valid edit: deployment mappings, external services, production-only inputs, recoverable state, rollout coordination and retained workflows. C7/C9 give reported failures at those boundaries; C3 shows why recovering service and preserving data require distinct endpoints. Finite replay, compiler checks, unit tests and successful deployment each expose only part of the obligation. No Nu advantage, agent repair effect or complete temporal oracle is established here.
