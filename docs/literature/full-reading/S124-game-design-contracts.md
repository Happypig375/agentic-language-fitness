## Study and positive result

B03/B05/B07/B08/B10 need direct evidence about executable contracts in an interactive game, including benefits that qualify a purely critical account of formal methods. The authors developed *Contract Princes*, a networked multiplayer real-time strategy game, predominantly through one implementer under simulated industrial conditions. Implementation took approximately 45 full-time days and produced about 400 source files. Approximately 140 contracts supplemented roughly 100 ordinary assertions. This is one development case, not a randomized comparison of teams, languages or architectures.

The main observed result is concrete: **49 of 80 reported semantic errors were detected by contracts**, with preconditions providing most contract detections. Contract-detected errors were more often classified as easy to locate, especially those detected by postconditions. The error log also records useful localization of broken assumptions and architecture refactoring after discrepancies were exposed. These are positive observations about the actual development process; they should not be erased because a causal comparison is absent.

The authors' three hypotheses have different dispositions:

The favorable diagnosis observation and the rejected second hypothesis coexist. The conclusion's use of significance language does not identify a reported statistical test, interval or preregistered criterion. The study supplies descriptive evidence and feasibility experience.

## What was implemented and observed

Contracts are C++ preprocessor/library checks on method entry and exit. The mechanism lacks direct class-invariant support, so invariant checks are folded into pre/postconditions. Contracts can be disabled at compilation. This is implementation-phase instrumentation, not a static proof of complete behavior or an architectural specification enforced across arbitrary event histories.

The game uses Crystal Space, a data-driven archetype/property system, and client/server ordering. The server authorizes commands and broadcasts advance-turn packets. Plans persist between turns and produce commands that change state; the architecture requires consistent ordering, batched state changes and independence of plans. This supplies a concrete predecessor for separating intent, commands and synchronized world updates. It is not evidence that one source language uniquely provides those capabilities.

Examples check an empty mesh list before synchronization, a movement intention when movement is possible, and a strict interior range for a geometry ratio. The movement condition is conditional: when movement is impossible, the implication can hold without a move intention. The prose's broader explanation must not turn this into an unconditional success requirement. The geometry example excludes its endpoints. Exact predicate meaning matters as much as whether a check executes.

The log records defect type, detection mechanism, location difficulty, repair action and an estimate of how readily a contract could have detected a non-contract error. Easy means immediately apparent from code; medium requires stack/value debugging; hard requires more extensive work; unresolved remains separate. Static compiler errors are described as outside the useful semantic-error analysis, despite the hypothesis discussion also mentioning compiler comparisons. No complete compiler/static-error denominator is reported alongside the eighty semantic errors.

## Figures and denominator limits

Figure 3 reports 45% precondition, 16% postcondition, 28% testing and 11% runtime detections. The paper's explicit 49/80 is the soundest contract-detection denominator; it is **not** sensitivity against all defects that existed, nor the percentage of defects uniquely detectable by contracts. Undetected faults are unknown, ordinary assertions and testing coexist, and the same underlying fault can trigger several nearby checks. The paper does not provide a public deduplication/adjudication procedure for the complete log.

Figure 4 reports location categories of 28% easy, 34% medium, 34% hard and 4% unresolved. Figure 5's retrospective contract-construction categories are 36% hard, 19% easy, 29% impossible and 16% medium. Figure 6 includes 5% flawed contracts, directly showing that specifications themselves can be wrong. Figure 7 assigns 62% of fixes to a small code change, with function/module/system refactoring and no action comprising the remainder. A quick repair and an easy diagnosis are different outcomes.

The chart data should not be reverse-engineered into a fictitious exact error log. Reading Figure 8 against its axis gives approximate integer groups `(easy, medium, hard, unresolved)` of preconditions `(11,18,6,1)`, postconditions `(9,3,1,0)`, testing `(1,3,17,1)` and runtime `(1,4,2,1)`. These sum to **79**, and the difficulty totals do not exactly reconcile with Figure 4. The crop and vector geometry confirm the displayed bars, not their underlying dataset. Figure 9 qualitatively supports the easier contract-detection pattern; Figure 10 supports the mixed detection of assumptions. Raw log access is needed to resolve the aggregate discrepancy. No corrected counts, inferential test or effect size is invented here.

## Temporal behavior, oracle validity and costs

Preconditions sometimes detect an error introduced earlier in the event chain rather than in the immediate caller. They can act as delayed checks of a predecessor's result. This helps explain useful diagnosis but also why a failed local check need not isolate its cause. Repeated nearby failures motivated refactoring; their predictive value was not independently tested.

Two reported incidents sharply limit what agreement with a contract means. In path finding, the implementation conformed to its design while that design failed the intended shortest-path requirement; strengthening checks of the same design did not locate a nonexistent implementation defect. In the architecture, a behavior directly changed object properties instead of routing changes through commands at the synchronization boundary. Contracts helped diagnosis but could not directly express the relevant cross-client ordering constraint in the chosen mechanism. These are concrete requirement/projection and temporal-coverage problems, not merely missing local assertions.

The paper argues that state-sequence synchronization contracts are too costly or unsuitable for the chosen lightweight approach. That is a limit of this study's instrumentation and design judgement. It is not a general impossibility result for executable temporal checking: S46/S47/S53 and [S123](S123-playspecs-trace-semantics.md) supply explicit later mechanisms with their own finite-observation limits. Conversely, their availability does not show they would have solved this historical case cheaply or completely.

Practical burdens are reported: cumbersome preprocessing, poor debugger integration, repeated header checks affecting build/runtime work, hard-to-express postconditions, and assumptions about third-party components that lack contracts. Some constraints were easier to express as unit tests. Contract failure could interrupt automated test coverage; defensive recovery and wrappers were proposed, with their own integration burden. The authors report no noticeable schedule delay and estimate reduced debugging time, but provide no measured net-time comparison or numeric overhead series. Disabling checks in a release changes runtime cost and detection opportunity together. Historical comments about C++ libraries and game practice are not contemporary performance measurements.
