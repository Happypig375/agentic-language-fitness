## Live questions and design

B01/B10/B11/B12 need evidence about what practitioners actually consult, which design properties they value and whether reported usefulness establishes future maintenance benefit. S158 supplies the first two through self-report. It does not observe development work, independently score quality or compare subsequent changes. Its four research questions concern attention to quality, recognition, guidance and information used in design decisions, at the class/package level of object-oriented software.

The authors used a Goal–Question–Metric mapping and a Qualtrics questionnaire: 29 topic questions, seven demographic questions and one final comment question. The topic questions comprise fifteen multiple-choice questions, one numeric question and thirteen free-text questions; the final comment makes fourteen free-text opportunities. Most ratings have five ordered categories with a neutral midpoint. Confidence has four categories; usefulness adds a not-used category. Do not merge nonuse with low utility or treat ordinal distances as calibrated quantities. Questions explicitly suggest several principles and tools; testing/TDD also emerges from unsolicited text, rather than from a dedicated testing intervention or direct testing question.

The June–July 2014 pilot invited ten people: six target practitioners, two researchers and two academics. It yielded four complete and two partial responses; the one uninterrupted timing was 36 minutes. Revisions clarified language, removed confusing optional boxes and an unfamiliar Law of Demeter question, and reduced some choices. The main survey ran July–December 2014. Recruitment used 48 personal/university contacts, 34 Scottish incubator contacts, social-network communities and, after two months, 275 authors with industrial affiliations found in 2009–2014 publications. Recipients could forward invitations. This is convenience/snowball sampling with an unknown invitation denominator; **102/275 is not a response rate**.

The paper reports 222 responses and categorizes 96 complete main responses, 93 blank, 23 partial and six pilot responses. These categories total **218**, leaving four unreconciled. The 23 partial responses were excluded using a judgment about insufficient answers. The earlier pilot account distinguishes four complete/two partial, while the analysis includes six pilot responses; question revisions and harmonization cannot be reconstructed without the release. The analyzed set is **102 practitioners** with current or previous industrial experience: 56 UK, forty elsewhere and six without location. Roles, languages and application backgrounds can overlap. Ninety-three report programming duties; only 97 supply years of experience. Individual plots have their own missingness.

Approximately 25,000 words of free text were coded through four iterative passes, using holistic, in-vivo, descriptive and contrast-oriented coding without a predetermined codebook. The tables count coded mentions within questions and present illustrative common or interesting responses. They are not independent events, population prevalences or exhaustive measures of importance. No independent coder agreement is reported; publishing raw responses was intended to aid traceability, not an observed reliability estimate here.

## Results retained, including qualifications and contrary accounts

| Finding in the selected edition | Evidence and interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Experience and review are central | Q1 puts personal experience ahead of peer, technical-lead/expert and team review; guidelines, metrics and tools are also used and valued. This is reported usefulness, not demonstrated expert accuracy or a comparison of review costs. |
| Correctness usually receives greater priority | Q6 has 83 very-important plus fifteen important ratings for correctness; design has 39 plus 53. Figure 7 says 99 answered both, two answered one each and one neither: 100 answers per outcome, not 102 complete paired answers. Q7 suggests roughly twice as much effort on correctness, but these are rough retrospective estimates of overlapping activities, not measured task times. |
| Good design is associated with change and comprehension | Free-text counts include maintainability/extensibility 31, clarity 28, modularity eighteen, simplicity/smallness eighteen and testability fourteen. These are participants' descriptions, not empirical effects of any particular implementation mechanism. Documentation is valued by some and considered replaceable by clear code by others. |
| Several OO guidelines have broad support | Q10 reports 84 positive interface ratings; Q18 reports 89 positive coupling ratings. Q20 has 75 positive cohesion ratings, while KF9/summary says 70%; 75 of the full 102 is about 73.5%, so the printed summary is not arithmetically reconciled here. Respondents also describe excessive interfaces as clutter and a change burden, and cohesion as difficult to define or inappropriate to maximize without context. |
| Inheritance and patterns are less uniformly endorsed | Inheritance has 55 positive and sixteen negative ratings. Accounts include reuse, substitution and flexibility, alongside preferences for composition and shallow/domain-appropriate hierarchies. Approximately 60% rate patterns positively, with common vocabulary as one benefit and overapplication as a cost. This is mixed practice evidence, not an experiment showing inheritance or patterns harmful. |
| Size and complexity attract attention without universal thresholds | Roughly 70–80% rate class/method size or complexity positively as considerations. Table 10 contains differing thresholds, often from one or two respondents, and a rendered method-size entry literally showing `<=0`; it is not silently corrected into a recommendation. Some respondents warn that extracting essential complexity can make the whole system harder to understand. |
| Refactoring competes with other work | Complexity, coupling, size and cohesion are prominent reported triggers. Commercial urgency and available time constrain action. Testing difficulty, coverage, mocks and TDD are mentioned as indicators or practices; no causal testing-to-design-quality effect or complete behavioral oracle is measured. |

Confidence is generally high, with uncertainty attributed to knowledge, requirements, alternative choices, future change and limited control. Those responses help characterize decisions faced by practitioners. They neither calibrate confidence nor demonstrate that a guideline improves those decisions.

Section 6 explores **51 plots**: seventeen questionnaire results against experience, main language and role. The authors inspect shapes visually instead of testing hypotheses, finding three conspicuous experience-related patterns and otherwise similar shapes. The plots use raw counts across small and unequal categories. Similar shapes do not establish subgroup equivalence or absence of language/experience effects. For confidence, the printed comparison is thirteen confident of 26 people with more than twenty years' experience, versus 26 of 71 with at most twenty years. This is descriptive and potentially confounded by responsibility and selection. The authors themselves note that experienced respondents valuing leads may be leads.

## Validity, released materials and transfer

The questionnaire and explicit research-question mapping make the measured constructs inspectable. Positive guideline support, practitioner qualifications and contrary comments must all survive synthesis. The authors explicitly acknowledge that the survey identifies indicators but does not determine their strength or connection to actual quality (especially pp. 34 and 40).

The sampling frame overrepresents accessible and academically connected practitioners. Self-selection, social desirability, retrospective estimates, suggested topics, fixed ordering and subjective coding limit inference. Checking similar response shapes does not repair selection bias. The response-total, pilot-inclusion and percentage discrepancies affect exact reconstruction; they do not erase the broad reported pattern. No claim of industry-wide prevalence, causal maintenance benefit, expert calibration, Nu/F# advantage or agent transfer follows.
