## What this study establishes

B03/B05/B07/B08/B10 need alternatives to treating functional worlds, ECS and ordinary objects as the whole design space. S68 supplies a useful navigation map: 108 language summaries, fourteen perspectives and a further synthesis around automated game design. The perspectives include structured analysis and patterns, assessment, mechanics, level generation, behavior, narrative, education, general game playing, game generation, scripting, model-driven engineering and metaprogramming. They overlap; they are not 108 independent comparisons or mutually exclusive architectures.

Its positive contribution is concrete breadth. Game languages already describe rules and resources, coordinate characters, generate and analyze content, support live interaction, and connect authoring to formal models. Some have practical users, shipped games, educational communities or maintained tools. The taxonomy makes those mechanisms easier to compare and exposes neighboring communities that a narrow programming-language query can miss. The survey does **not** itself test whether adopting a language reduces maintenance time, increases correctness or improves player experience.

The organizing hypotheses favor language support and DSL automation, while acknowledging construction, training, evolution, tool, community and legacy-support costs. The proposed relation between faster iteration and better games is a research motivation, not a measured monotonic effect. Figure 1 separates design intention from the experience players actually obtain. A valid source program or generated playable level need not deliver either the designer's intended behavior or the intended experience.

## Search, classification and denominator

The Google Scholar narrow query combines `"domain specific language"` with `"game development" OR "game design"`, yielding roughly 400 results. The wide query combines `language AND games` while excluding eight noisy terms concerning language learning, body/sign language, game-based learning, beer games and gamification. It yielded roughly 17,500 results, later described as more than 16,000; only its first 1,000 were screened. Queries ran on 2–8 March 2018, with citation retrieval through 21 March. Hand additions and later access dates extend into 2019/2020. These are retrieval stages and occurrences, not a single deduplicated independent-study denominator.

Selection emphasizes written, fixed-structure language approaches directly relevant to games. English-only coverage, excluded query terms, ranked prefixes, search-date limits and manual additions constrain recall. The paper explicitly records departures from the protocol and query-origin/rank labels in the catalog. Generic high-performance computing and game theory are outside its focus; commercial games, engines and tools were not directly sampled as a population. Our Nu background is broader than the survey's particular definition of automated game content, which excludes engine/library development.

The coding distinguishes application/genre/generic scope, framework/tool/engine form and research/education/practice context. Objectives such as communication, illustration, maintenance, productivity, quality and reuse are nonexclusive. Syntax, scoping, conditions, recurrence, modularity, domain features and feedback/mixed-initiative/live interfaces describe capabilities; their presence is not an outcome measure. Research categories extend an existing classification with practice, experience and tutorial material. No independent double-coding or inter-rater agreement procedure is described. Author feedback and proofreading do not establish such reliability.

The graph omits disconnected publications, and its force-directed layout admits different embeddings. The author's interpretation of fragmented communities is a useful discovery hypothesis, not proof of missing conceptual overlap or field-wide isolation. The apparent post-2015 publication decline is explicitly attributed to the query date and ranking that favors older results, rather than a decline of the field.

## Evidence and adoption claims

On p. 27, **only eleven of the 108 language summaries have at least one publication classified as evaluation research**. This is a statement about the author's classification and sampled literature. It is not eleven controlled developer studies, an estimated success rate, or a finding that the other languages do not work. Evaluation-labelled examples include player/game quality and educational outcomes as well as development concerns. Primary methods are needed before importing any effect estimate.

Tables 25/26 associate sustained languages with multiple contributors, years, venues, practical application, released materials and examples. These are qualitative observations and selected examples, not a causal model or longitudinal estimate of adoption. The reported availability of supplementary material for roughly half the languages is dated. The associated maintenance, training and collaboration costs are plausible and explicitly acknowledged but not measured comparatively here. S64's separate industry live-tooling survey offers a complementary practice route; its evidence should not be replaced with this academic sample.

## Three-criterion disposition and continuation

**Unique:** broad claims about declarative game rules, coordination, analysis and live tools have substantial precedents. S68 expands the set of rivals; exact Nu/D1 priority remains unconfirmed. **Valuable:** practical capabilities and some evaluated applications are concrete, while their net maintenance value and transfer to Nu or coding agents remain unmeasured. Sparse evaluation does not establish lack of value. **Scientifically valid:** the mapping supports discovery and explicit distinctions, not a validated causal comparison or an independently correct behavioral oracle.

**Continuation reconciled, 2026-10-02:** S123/S124, S121/S122, S223/S224 and S225’s live game-adaptation method are now primary-read. Ceptre and the earlier formal MM account remain conditional resource/temporal-method dependencies, not automatically required next papers. The acquired S156 comparative-maintenance method is now reconstructed; the wider survey next prioritizes game-agent evaluation coverage. Nu transfer and professional maintenance benefit remain empirical questions, separately from unread primary methods. All experimental holds and zero new allocation remain.
